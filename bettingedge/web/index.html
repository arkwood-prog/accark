How a price is made

Every number on this page comes from the chain below. No step is a black box, and each one can be inspected in the other tabs.

  1. Fit the goal model. A Dixon-Coles bivariate Poisson is fitted by maximum likelihood to every result in the history window. Each team gets an attack and a defence rating; the league gets a home-advantage term and a low-score correlation term (ρ) that fixes the well-known Poisson misfit on 0-0, 1-0, 0-1 and 1-1. Matches are weighted by an exponential time decay, so last month counts for more than last year.
  2. Build a score matrix. For each fixture the model produces the full joint distribution over scorelines — the probability of every 0-0, 2-1, 3-2 and so on.
  3. Derive every market from that one object. Match result, double chance, over/under and both-teams-to-score are all just sums over regions of the same matrix, so they can never contradict each other.
  4. Strip the bookmaker's margin. Prices imply probabilities summing to more than 100%. Shin's method removes that overround while accounting for the favourite-longshot bias, giving the market's honest opinion.
  5. Blend model with market. The two opinions are averaged in logit space. Closing prices are hard to beat, so the default leans on the market and treats the model as a tilt — the "model weight" slider controls exactly how much.
  6. Re-anchor the score matrix. The blended probabilities are pushed back into the joint distribution by iterative proportional fitting, so correct scores and same-game combinations inherit the market-anchored view.
  7. Measure the edge. Expected value is p × odds − 1 against the best price available. Only selections clearing the threshold survive.
  8. Build the slips. Singles, then doubles, trebles and accumulators from different matches. Same-match legs are priced from the joint distribution instead of being multiplied, because they are correlated.
  9. Size the bets. Fractional Kelly with per-bet caps and a slate-wide exposure ceiling, since a Saturday card does not settle one bet at a time.

What would make this wrong

Stated plainly, because a model that hides its failure modes is a liability.

  • Goals-only modelling knows nothing about injuries, suspensions, rotation before a cup tie, or a manager sacked on Thursday. The market knows all of it.
  • A price you saw is not a price you can get. Edges built on the best quote across many books shrink or vanish at the book you actually hold an account with.
  • Backtest ROI on a few hundred bets is noise. Closing-line value and log-loss against the market are the honest tests, which is why they are reported.
  • Accumulators multiply the bookmaker's margin as well as the odds. They are in here because they were asked for and because the maths is worth showing — they are not where the edge is.
